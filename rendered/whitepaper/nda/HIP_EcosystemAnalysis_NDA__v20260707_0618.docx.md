1. The Thesis
Forty funded companies operate in the household market today. Each owns a slice of household context: finance, or location, or health, or care, or the calendar. None owns the layer beneath the slice, the household identity, persistent cross-domain memory, role and permission model, and institutional-grade consent that the slice needs to fully actualize.
The wall is structural, and it is the same wall for all of them. A finance app cannot see the eldercare context that should change a budget. A safety app cannot see the custody schedule that should change an alert. A tutor cannot see the parent's goals or the second child's history. Each firm is capped not by its product but by the absence of a household substrate it cannot economically build alone, because building household identity, cross-domain memory, and enclave-grade consent is capital-intensive, privacy-fraught, and not any of their core competence.
HIP is that substrate. HIP provides the household operating system: identity, memory, permissions, consent, and operator-edge custody, as a governed kernel. Provide that kernel, and each of these forty firms actualizes into something it structurally cannot become on its own. This is the AWS, Stripe, and Plaid pattern applied to the household: nobody builds their own rails, they rent the substrate and focus on their slice.
The opportunity is operator-scale, and it starts as a floor, not a forecast. HIP reaches households through cable and broadband operators, a base of roughly 80 million US cable-broadband homes attached to an industry that is losing subscribers and has run out of new ARPU levers. A single AI-service line across that base is a $4.8 billion to $14.4 billion annual revenue category before one app rides on top. Section 2 sizes the full stack, the operator base first, then the platform economy that compounds above it. Everything in this document sits on that floor, and one layer of the stack, eldercare, adds a reimbursed Medicare path on top of consumer demand, sized separately in Section 2.
This document analyzes all forty firms on a single frame and sorts them into the strategic map that follows. It is confidential because it contains the partner economics, the competitive assessment, and the acquisition framing that the public and investor versions do not.

2. The Opportunity, Sized
HIP has two markets, and both are sized here. The first is the subscription market, households paying for HIP through operators, built bottom-up in the Financial Annex from a real operator base and surfaced here as Layer 0, the floor beneath everything. The second is the platform market, the economy that rides on HIP, sized as four layers ordered from hard to emergent. The full opportunity is one stack, an operator base carrying four platform layers on top, and it is stated that way deliberately. A single inflated figure invites dismissal; a layered figure lets the reader bank the floor and weigh the upside separately.
2.1 Layer 0: The operator base and the AI-service revenue line (hard, the floor beneath everything)
Before the platform economy there is the channel that carries it. HIP reaches households through cable and broadband operators, and that operator base is both the distribution and the first revenue line. It is the hardest number in this section, resting on subscriber counts that public operators report every quarter rather than on adoption curves.
The reachable base: roughly 80 million US cable broadband households. Comcast serves approximately 31 million broadband subscribers, and Charter approximately 36 million once its FCC-approved Cox acquisition closes, the two together near 67.7 million, the same industry scale the Financial Annex models. Optimum adds roughly 4 million, and the Tier 2 operators, Cable One, Astound, WOW, Breezeline, Midco, TDS, Consolidated, altafiber, and Mediacom, carry the rest to a defensible cable core near 80 million. If operator-edge custody extends to telco fiber, the all-broadband ceiling is roughly 120 million.
The timing is the operator's problem, and HIP answers it. Cable broadband is under structural pressure. Comcast and Charter each lost subscribers again in the first quarter of 2026, both are down more than a million from their 2023 peaks, EBITDA is compressing, and broadband is now roughly 40 percent of Charter's revenue with no video business left to lean on. Operators have no new ARPU lever that does not require a price war they are already losing. HIP is a new AI-service line that monetizes assets they already own, the customer-premises equipment, the edge, and the subscriber relationship, and it adds retention against fiber and fixed-wireless switching, which for an operator shedding subscribers is worth as much as the revenue itself.

2.3 Layer 2: Eldercare and memory care, the anchor vertical (hard, demographically locked)
Eldercare is not one category among nine. It is the anchor, because its demand is demographically locked rather than adoption-dependent: the US 85+ population goes from 6.3 million in 2024 to 14.4 million by 2040, and 7.4 million Americans already live with Alzheimer's dementia. The demand compounds regardless of any assumption in this model.
The defensible number: roughly $30B by 2030. US tech-enabled home eldercare, anchored to the remote-patient-monitoring software and services forecast of $28.6 billion by 2030 (33.6 percent CAGR). That sits inside a $162 billion US home healthcare market (2024), growing toward $381 billion by 2033, inside a $540 billion-plus total long-term care market. HIP addresses the coordination and intelligence slice, not the whole, and saying so precisely is what makes the number credible.
The demand is proven, the revenue category is still forming. 59 million US family caregivers provide $1.01 trillion in unpaid labor annually. Families already pay $6,200 per month for assisted living and $25 to $48 per month for basic monitoring subscriptions. The willingness to pay is established at both ends; what does not yet exist is the household AI coordination layer between them. That is the gap HIP's eldercare vertical fills, and it is why eldercare is the vertical most likely to pull HIP's first paid agents into real revenue.
HIP's line inside the category, sized. Anchor to the demographically locked core: roughly 3 million US cable-broadband households actively managing dementia or 85-plus care today, conservative against the 7.4 million dementia population nationally, doubling toward 6 million by 2040 as the 85-plus cohort doubles. At $40 per month of coordination spend and 35 percent adoption, the eldercare flow through HIP is $0.50 billion per year today. What makes eldercare the anchor is the capture mode: it is the vertical where HIP is the billed coordination service, not a 17.5 percent skim on someone else's app, which is why its take multiplies from the marketplace floor.

2.4 Layer 3: Ecosystem expansion (directional)

The real platform expands on two axes: existing categories deepen as apps compose on shared context, and entirely new categories appear that do not exist today, the next hundred firms building on a substrate that makes each one cheap to build. This layer is directional by nature and carries the adaptive caveat: it is the bet the hundreds of millions already invested in these forty firms are making, that the household becomes an AI-mediated operating environment, and it is why they expect a market larger than any static snapshot implies.

2.5 Layer 4: Robotics adjacency (emergent, modeled attach)
Home robotics is a second economy forming on the same substrate: not an app category but a device class that will need household identity, memory, permissions, and context rather than each robot running an isolated stack. The academic literature already documents the gap, smart-home interoperability and multi-user access-control failures that a household permission layer resolves.
The defensible anchor: $40B global consumer robotics by 2030. Consumer robotics is forecast to reach roughly $40 billion by 2030 (25 percent CAGR) and 134 million annual shipments, while US smart homes reach $84 billion by 2030. Real today is vacuums (20.6 million units, $9.3 billion in 2024), mowers, security devices, and companion niches. A household AI substrate is not yet a reported market category, but a 5 to 10 percent software and platform attach rate on 2030 consumer robotics implies a $2 to $4 billion annual platform opportunity, before broader smart-home and care-device adjacency.
Stated straight, because a reviewer will test it. The capital validates the direction: robotics venture funding went from $7.2 billion in 2024 to $18.8 billion in 2026 year-to-date, and robot-foundation-model platforms (Skild at $1.5 billion, Physical Intelligence at $2 billion) prove investors are betting on a platform layer, just not a household-identity-specific one yet. The humanoid-in-every-home layer is exciting, funded, and forecast-dependent; early units exist (1X NEO at $20,000 or $499 per month, Unitree G1 near $16,000) but run with human remote supervision. This layer is the option, not the plan. It is included because a platform substrate that ignores the device economy forming on top of it is under-imagining the opportunity, which is the error this section was revised to correct.

3. The Analytical Frame
Every firm is scored on four questions, applied identically so the analysis is comparable rather than anecdotal:
1. What slice do they own? Their current moat, the thing they do well today.
2. What is the wall? The specific thing they cannot do because they lack the household substrate.
3. What does HIP unlock? The actualization, stated concretely, not as a better experience.
4. Why HIP specifically? Why not build it themselves, use Apple or Google, or a cloud identity provider. This is the discipline question that keeps the thesis honest.
The answer to the fourth question lands in the same place every time: Apple and Google will not build the household custody layer because their model depends on holding the data; a cloud identity provider cannot, because it has no household trust boundary or operator-edge presence. HIP occupies the position none of the incumbents will take and none of the forty can afford to build.

5. Why the Ecosystem Thrives on HIP
The sort above shows what each firm cannot do alone. This section makes the affirmative case: on HIP, these firms do not merely get unstuck individually, they become collectively more valuable. HIP does not aggregate a fragmented market; it creates value that does not exist today, because a shared household substrate produces network effects no single firm can access. Four mechanisms drive it.
4.1 Cross-app context compounds
Every application on HIP becomes more valuable as other applications join, because they share the household graph. A finance app can see, with consent, the eldercare cost that should change a budget. A tutor sees the calendar. A safety app sees the custody schedule. Standalone, none of these firms can offer cross-domain awareness; on HIP, all of them can, and each new app raises the value of every app already present. This is a genuine network effect, the property that separates a thriving platform from a directory of point solutions.
4.2 The developer flywheel
More households give apps more reason to build on HIP; a richer catalog gives households more reason to join; the loop compounds. The shared identity, consent, and memory layer is what makes each new app cheap to build, developers do not rebuild the substrate, so the cost of ecosystem entry falls and the flywheel accelerates. The forty firms analyzed here are not a fixed market to be divided; they are the first turn of a flywheel that lowers the cost of the next hundred.
4.3 Consent is the enabler, not just the shield
This is the mechanism that makes the ecosystem legally and commercially possible. HIP's operator-edge custody is not only a privacy defense. It is what permits cross-app data flow that would otherwise be a liability no firm would touch. The bank app can share with the eldercare app because HIP governs the consent, auditable, revocable, scoped. Without a governed substrate, cross-app sharing in the household is a regulatory and reputational risk that kills the network effect before it starts. HIP's trust architecture is precisely what lets the ecosystem thrive: it converts privacy from a cost center into the enabling layer for everything above it.
4.4 Categories come alive, not just firms
The clearest way to see the ecosystem thrive is at the category level. Each of the nine verticals, individually a set of fragmented point solutions today, becomes a coordinated sub-ecosystem on a shared household graph. The four categories where the mesh is most vivid are shown in full below; the rest in brief.
Family coordination  (Cozi, Maple, FamilyWall, OurHome, Nori)
Today these five compete for the same household calendar, each rebuilding identity, roles, and recurring context from scratch, each a walled silo. On HIP they stop competing for the substrate and start composing on it. Cozi's calendar, FamilyWall's shared docs, and Nori's proactive agent all read and write the same governed household graph, so a school event entered once propagates to every surface, child permissions set once apply everywhere, and the recurring context each app rebuilds today is built once and shared. The category stops being five thin apps fighting over the kitchen calendar and becomes a coordinated household-operations mesh where each app is more useful because the others are present.
Eldercare  (CareLinx, Honor, Papa, Ianacare, Zingage, Lotsa Helping Hands)
Eldercare is the most fragmented category and the one that thrives most visibly on a shared substrate. Today a caregiver handoff loses context, a companionship visit starts cold, a family caregiver and a paid agency operate from separate notes, and the payer sees none of it. On HIP these six become a coordinated care mesh over one permissioned household graph: Honor's caregiver and Papa's companion arrive with the same authorized context, Ianacare's family caregiver and Zingage's agency share a single source of truth without collapsing their distinct roles, CareLinx's benefits and Lotsa's volunteers attach to the same household with correct consent boundaries. The care improves not because any one firm got better, but because the household finally has one memory that all of them can safely read and write. This is a category that cannot exist in coordinated form without a governed substrate, and comes alive with one.
Health data  (Human API, 1upHealth, Particle Health, Validic, b.well)
These five are already infrastructure, they move health data between institutions. What they cannot do is turn that data into governed household decisions, because interoperability plumbing has no home-side identity, consent, or reasoning layer. On HIP they become the input rails to a household health intelligence that families actually control: 1upHealth and Particle bring the clinical records, Validic brings the device streams, Human API brings consumer-permissioned data, and HIP is the layer that interprets, routes, and escalates within the family, under household consent rather than institutional access. The rails were always there; HIP is what makes them add up to something a household can use, which is the value the rails alone could never capture.
Household finance  (Monarch, Copilot, YNAB, Plaid, MX)
Finance splits cleanly into rails (Plaid, MX) and household relationships (Monarch, Copilot, YNAB), and both halves are capped by the same missing context. Plaid and MX move money data but have no household model; Monarch and YNAB own budgeting behavior but are blind to the eldercare cost, the school expense, the insurance change that actually drives a household's finances. On HIP the rails feed a household-aware financial layer where a budget responds to a real care commitment, a coverage change surfaces in the finance app because the insurance vertical is on the same graph, and money decisions carry the full household context they need. Finance stops being a walled vertical and becomes a household function informed by every other function.
Smart home  (Home Assistant, SmartThings, Matter/Thread, Josh.ai)
Device control reaches hundreds of millions of homes but stays context-blind. On HIP the device layer becomes the execution surface for household intelligence, actions tied to family context, roles, and routines rather than dumb automation. HIP is the brain behind the rails these platforms already own.
Family safety  (Life360, Bark, Qustodio, Circle (Aura))
Each owns one safety signal, location, online activity, network, with no shared household context to make the signal smart. On HIP the signals compose: a location alert reads the custody schedule, a monitoring flag reads the child's roles and routines. Safety becomes context-aware rather than a stream of disconnected alerts.
Education  (Khanmigo, IXL, Sizzle)
Tutors and practice engines are context-blind to the household, blind to parent goals, sibling history, and household routine. On HIP they share a household learning memory, so tutoring and practice compound across children and time rather than resetting each session.
Insurance  (Policygenius, Lemonade, Hippo)
Insurance sees data only inside its own workflows and sells transactionally. On HIP it becomes continuous household risk management, authorized signals from the household graph that detect coverage gaps and prevent losses, tied into the finance vertical on the same substrate.
Emerging household AI  (Ohai, Nori, Hearth, Luffu, Honeydew)
These are building toward the household layer directly. On HIP the ones that partner become premium surfaces on a substrate they do not have to fund, and the category stops being a race to rebuild identity and custody and becomes a race to build the best experience on top of a shared one.
The through-line: none of these categories can exist in coordinated form without a governed household substrate, and each comes alive with one. That is the difference between a platform that thrives and a market that is merely served.

9. Partner and Marketplace Economics
This section is NDA-restricted. It applies HIP's marketplace take-rate model to the tenant and partner classes above.
The take rate is empirically anchored. HIP's marketplace fee of 17.5 percent (mode) sits inside a benchmarked band: Roku charges 20 percent on transactional apps, the closest connected-TV-operator analog; Salesforce AppExchange and Shopify charge 15 percent for governed ISV distribution; AWS Marketplace charges 3 percent for pure infrastructure distribution; Apple, Google, and the consoles charge 30 percent. HIP at 17.5 percent sits between the operator-platform comp (Roku) and the governed-ISV comp (Salesforce), deliberately below the Apple ceiling to be developer-friendly.

The two-sided structure. Tenant firms (the twenty that own a household relationship) are the app-layer revenue: they build on HIP, HIP takes the marketplace fee on what they sell into the household. Partner firms (the fifteen connectors) are the integration substrate: HIP pays or revenue-shares to bring their data in, and that connectivity is what makes the tenant apps valuable. The marketplace only works with both sides present, which is why the forty-firm ecosystem, not any single app, is the moat.
Discipline: marketplace is upside, not base case. Consistent with the financial model, marketplace revenue is modeled as an explicitly labeled upside band, not base-case revenue. The subscription business must stand alone. The marketplace compounds behind subscriber scale, and requires the household base before the developer base, the chicken-and-egg the anchor-tenant strategy is designed to break: HIP seeds the first applications internally, the ecosystem follows the households.
