Bottom line
The design is not yet safe enough to support its central promises.
Three problems are fundamental:
	1	“Confirmed” conflates truth, consent, authority, and provenance. A person can confirm a false fact, refuse to confirm a true one, be coerced into confirming, or lack authority over a relational fact.
	2	“Recognize and route, never adjudicate” is too passive for imminent danger. You should avoid deciding whether an accusation is true, but you cannot avoid assessing immediate risk.
	3	Strict per-member isolation is not currently true. With operator-controlled hardware, operator-accessible processing, no per-member cryptographic keys, and an abusive account holder, you have policy isolation—not defensible cryptographic isolation.
The rest of the system is built on those unresolved foundations.

1. Confirmation subroutine
Where it breaks
A. There often is no single authorized confirmer
Many household statements are not facts owned by one person.
Examples:
	•	“Mom and Dad are having marital problems.”
	•	“Susan takes better care of Dad than Michael does.”
	•	“Dad becomes confused after dinner.”
	•	“The family always spends Christmas in Vermont.”
	•	“Ben is frightened of his stepfather.”
	•	“Grandma wanted the house to remain in the family.”
	•	“The children are closer to their mother.”
Who confirms these?
	•	The subject may not know.
	•	Multiple subjects may disagree.
	•	The statement may describe a relationship rather than an individual.
	•	The author may be the only witness.
	•	The subject may have an incentive to deny it.
	•	Nobody may have authority to declare one interpretation canonical.
You need to stop modeling confirmation as a transition from unconfirmed fact → confirmed fact. The proper object is an attributed claim:
Susan asserts that Dad becomes confused after dinner, based on her observations during the past month.
Dad can confirm or dispute Susan’s description, but he cannot erase her observation. Susan cannot establish Dad’s internal state. The system should preserve both perspectives without merging them into one supposed truth.
B. Confirmation does not establish truth
A confirmation establishes only that:
	•	a particular actor,
	•	at a particular time,
	•	under uncertain conditions,
	•	affirmed a particular representation.
It does not establish objective accuracy.
Your KYC analogy is misleading. KYC generally attempts to validate identity or documents against external evidence. You are asking family members to validate ambiguous social reality. That is closer to provenance management, belief revision, multi-informant assessment, and noisy-label learning than KYC.
Research on surrogate decision-making shows the problem plainly: in one systematic review, surrogates predicted patient treatment preferences correctly only about 68% of the time, and patient designation of the surrogate did not materially solve the problem. Accuracy was particularly poor in dementia scenarios.
C. “Never dead-ends” is false
A fact can have no reachable authorized confirmer:
	•	The person is dead.
	•	The person is estranged and will never enroll.
	•	The person refuses to interact with the system.
	•	The claim concerns a visitor or former household member.
	•	The person’s identity cannot be resolved.
	•	Two legal guardians dispute who has authority.
	•	Capacity fluctuates and no valid surrogate exists.
	•	A child ages into independent authority but rejects the historical record.
	•	A jointly held fact has no single decisive subject.
	•	The proposed confirmer is the alleged abuser.
	•	The only witness must remain anonymous for safety.
“Pending indefinitely” is a workflow state, but it is also a semantic dead end. Pretending otherwise means your process will keep pursuing people who have declined, become unreachable, or should not be contacted.
You need explicit terminal states such as:
	•	DECLINED
	•	UNREACHABLE
	•	NO_AUTHORITY_RESOLVED
	•	CONTESTED
	•	AUTHOR_ONLY
	•	PROXY_ATTESTED
	•	EXPIRED
	•	WITHDRAWN
	•	UNSAFE_TO_CONFIRM
	•	SUBJECT_DECEASED
A system that “never gives up” can become coercive.
D. Confirmation requests can themselves leak private information
Suppose Susan privately says:
“I think Michael is drinking again.”
Sending Michael a confirmation request tells him:
	•	someone made the claim,
	•	the system retained it,
	•	someone in the household may be discussing him,
	•	potentially which person said it.
Even a carefully worded prompt may reveal the existence of the allegation. Re-routing through another family member makes the privacy violation worse.
The confirmation process therefore needs its own information-flow policy. Contextual integrity evaluates privacy through the subject, sender, recipient, information type, and transmission principle—not simply whether the subject eventually approved the data.
E. A malicious narrator can weaponize the queue
An abusive or antagonistic narrator can create hundreds of claims:
	•	“She is mentally unstable.”
	•	“He forgets his medication.”
	•	“She cannot manage money.”
	•	“He lies about where he goes.”
Even if these remain “unconfirmed,” they can:
	•	burden the target with confirmation requests,
	•	establish a derogatory latent profile,
	•	influence inferred risk,
	•	contaminate retrieval or summarization,
	•	become discoverable records,
	•	reveal that the target is resisting “confirmation.”
This is a reputation attack and denial-of-service attack against the confirmation mechanism.
F. Confirmation fatigue will degrade meaning
Repeated prompts produce habituation and low-attention approvals. Research on repeated consent interactions has found diminished attention through habituation; human-in-the-loop systems must also model noisy and abstaining annotators rather than presuming an always-available reliable oracle.
A person tapping “yes” after the fifth reminder may be clearing a queue, not deliberating.
Required correction
Separate at least five dimensions:
	1	Provenance: Who stated or inferred it?
	2	Epistemic status: Asserted, observed, corroborated, disputed, inferred.
	3	Storage permission: May the system retain it?
	4	Audience: Who may retrieve it, and for what purpose?
	5	Freshness: When does it require revalidation?
“Confirmed” should not be one field.

2. Seeding and onboarding
Where it breaks
A. The narrator becomes the constitutional founder of the household
The narrator controls:
	•	who is introduced first,
	•	how relationships are framed,
	•	which traditions count,
	•	which conflicts are omitted,
	•	who is characterized as dependent,
	•	who is described as trustworthy,
	•	which vocabulary becomes the graph ontology.
That first framing will anchor later interpretation even if claims remain formally unconfirmed.
A divorced father might describe his teenage daughter as “withdrawn and rebellious.” She might describe herself as avoiding an emotionally unsafe home. Those are not two candidate values for the same field. They are incompatible frames with different power implications.
The narrator’s claims must therefore be quarantined from personalization about other people until each person establishes an independent profile. Otherwise the narrator has already shaped the system before confirmation occurs.
B. “Told with pride” is not neutral
Starting with pride and warmth may improve engagement. It may also lower defenses and increase disclosure before users understand the system’s persistence, legal exposure, or operator access.
Motivational interviewing supports autonomy, collaboration, nonjudgment, and reflective engagement. But MI is not a collection trick. Its own professional description warns that it requires practice and discipline and should not be used as a technique to get people to do something.
Using MI-like warmth to increase the yield of an intimate household profile risks becoming manipulative unless the system repeatedly and visibly explains:
	•	what is being retained,
	•	who can access it,
	•	what will not be retained,
	•	how it can be deleted,
	•	what the operator can technically see,
	•	what legal process could compel.
C. Sensitive topics will not obey your zone order
Abuse, addiction, grief, sexuality, financial control, self-harm, and family conflict may emerge during the “warm” zone.
Examples:
	•	“Our Sunday dinners were important until Dad started throwing things.”
	•	“We always visit Grandma because Uncle James won’t let her leave.”
	•	“The children know not to wake Mom when she has been drinking.”
The safety and privacy systems must be operational from the first utterance. You cannot treat the first zones as low-risk.
D. Voice-first onboarding excludes and exposes people
Voice-first creates concrete failures:
	•	A person with aphasia, hearing loss, speech impairment, or strong accent is misrecognized.
	•	A cognitively impaired person agrees without understanding.
	•	Another household member is within earshot.
	•	The narrator answers for someone who is present.
	•	A visitor or domestic worker is recorded as background speech.
	•	Speaker identification assigns the claim to the wrong member.
	•	A replayed or synthesized voice “confirms” a claim.
Research on multi-user smart homes has repeatedly found that owners and secondary users have different levels of control even though both generate data, and that current smart-home designs struggle with those interpersonal privacy tensions.
Voice should be an interface option, not the identity or consent foundation.
E. Custodian confirmation is not an independent check
Making the custodian confirm facts they originally supplied adds friction but not epistemic independence.
Susan states, “Dad wants me to handle all his finances.” Susan, acting as custodian, confirms, “Yes, Dad wants me to handle all his finances.”
The second interaction does nothing to mitigate exploitation.
For people with limited capacity, the first presumption should be supported decision-making, not substitution. The Administration for Community Living describes supported decision-making as retaining control with the individual while providing tailored assistance. Capacity should also be treated as decision-specific rather than a permanent global designation.
Where a surrogate is required, you need:
	•	a legally or procedurally defensible basis for authority,
	•	scope-specific authority,
	•	the dependent’s assent where possible,
	•	respect for dissent,
	•	conflict-of-interest checks,
	•	escalation when the surrogate’s interests diverge.
AMA guidance explicitly recognizes substituted judgment, best-interest standards, state-law variation, assent, and circumstances in which a surrogate’s request should not be honored.
F. There is no “complete question set”
A complete household model does not exist.
Households include:
	•	roommates,
	•	multigenerational families,
	•	divorced and blended families,
	•	polyamorous relationships,
	•	foster children,
	•	domestic workers,
	•	rotating caregivers,
	•	college students who live elsewhere part-time,
	•	deceased people who remain important,
	•	pets whose care is distributed,
	•	relatives who have authority but do not reside there.
A universal complete questionnaire will embed cultural and family-structure assumptions. You need extensible domains and user-created meanings, not a closed interview canon.

3. Boundary manager
The basic flaw: engagement is not consent
“Earn depth” is better than indiscriminate interrogation, but it still treats behavioral engagement as authorization.
A person may answer at length because they:
	•	believe the system is ephemeral,
	•	think the narrator or account holder will see the answer,
	•	feel pressured to be cooperative,
	•	are lonely,
	•	are manic, intoxicated, cognitively impaired, or distressed,
	•	misunderstand how the information will be used.
Conversely, short answers may reflect:
	•	communication style,
	•	autism or neurodivergence,
	•	fatigue,
	•	aphasia,
	•	language mismatch,
	•	fear of being overheard,
	•	depression,
	•	intimidation by someone nearby.
The system cannot reliably infer “permission to deepen” from conversational behavior.
The creepiness objective is underspecified
“Feels like it knows you, not studies you” is a product aspiration, not an enforceable rule.
Creepiness depends on:
	•	who gathered the information,
	•	how it was gathered,
	•	when it resurfaces,
	•	who else is present,
	•	the purpose for which it is used,
	•	whether the person expected the flow.
That is precisely why contextual integrity is stronger than a scalar “depth” model. Privacy is about appropriate flows, not simply quantity or sensitivity.
A birthday preference resurfaced during a private conversation may feel helpful. The same preference spoken aloud while an estranged spouse is present may be a violation.
Behavioral backoff can become hidden psychological profiling
To decide whether someone is withdrawing, the system must analyze:
	•	answer length,
	•	latency,
	•	topic avoidance,
	•	sentiment,
	•	voice characteristics,
	•	interaction history.
That analysis is itself intimate profiling. You risk collecting a second-order record—“topics Ben avoids,” “Susan becomes tense when finances are discussed”—that is more sensitive than the original information.
The boundary manager must not silently write those observations into durable memory.
The design needs explicit controls
Behavioral cues can supplement, but not replace:
	•	“Do not ask me about this again.”
	•	“This conversation is private and should not be remembered.”
	•	“Pause memory for ten minutes.”
	•	“Only use this to answer me now.”
	•	“Show me what you learned from that conversation.”
	•	“Delete the inferred preference.”
	•	“Do not mention this when other people are present.”
Without visible controls, “backs off at withdrawal” is paternalistic inference masquerading as consent.

4. Safety layer
“Recognize and route, never adjudicate” is internally contradictory
To recognize and route, the system must determine at least:
	•	whether the statement concerns harm,
	•	whether the speaker is the subject or a third party,
	•	whether danger is current,
	•	whether the person is capable of acting,
	•	whether the setting is safe,
	•	which resource is appropriate,
	•	whether emergency intervention may be necessary.
That is adjudication of risk, even if it is not adjudication of truth or guilt.
The correct division is:
Do not adjudicate whether an accusation is true. Do assess whether there is a credible, immediate safety risk requiring action.
Route-only can cause harm
Cases where referral alone is inadequate:
	•	“I took all the pills ten minutes ago.”
	•	“He is downstairs with a gun.”
	•	“The baby is not breathing.”
	•	“My husband is strangling my mother.”
	•	“I am about to drive into traffic.”
	•	A child explicitly says they are currently locked in a room without food.
	•	An elder calls for help while another person can be heard threatening them.
Current 988 protocols require structured safety assessment and clear intervention parameters at the highest levels of risk. Emergency services are intended as a last, least-invasive option, but passive referral is not treated as sufficient when harm is imminent or in progress.
You need at least three operational classes:
State
Appropriate behavior
Distress without identified imminent danger
Listen, offer choices, offer private human support, do not interrogate
Possible danger or ambiguous risk
Minimal, direct safety check; establish whether the person can safely continue
Imminent or in-progress danger
Collaborative least-invasive intervention; emergency escalation when necessary
“Do not capture it” is technically implausible
Before the system decides not to store the disclosure, it may already exist in:
	•	microphone buffers,
	•	speech-to-text transcripts,
	•	model context,
	•	diagnostic logs,
	•	crash dumps,
	•	telemetry,
	•	abuse-detection traces,
	•	backups,
	•	operator support tools,
	•	observability systems.
You need a defined ephemeral safety-processing path, not a policy statement that the fact is not captured.
That path must specify:
	•	what temporarily exists,
	•	in which memory domains,
	•	maximum retention,
	•	which logs are suppressed,
	•	whether redacted event metadata remains,
	•	whether an emergency action is auditable,
	•	who can access that audit trail.
Not storing anything also causes continuity failure
Suppose a teenager discloses escalating self-harm three times over two weeks. If every interaction is discarded, each conversation starts from zero.
Suppose an elder repeatedly says a caregiver is withholding food. Complete non-retention prevents pattern recognition, safety planning, and continuity of support.
The solution cannot simply be “store it” because the account holder may be the abuser. You need a safe storage architecture—potentially a member-controlled encrypted safety record or no durable continuity at all. Under your current key architecture, you do not have a trustworthy location for it.
The account-holder-is-the-abuser case is not merely open
It invalidates several guarantees.
An abusive account holder may be able to:
	•	remove the victim’s access,
	•	reset credentials,
	•	inspect notifications,
	•	access router or operator logs,
	•	monitor device presence,
	•	power off or destroy the appliance,
	•	compel the victim to authenticate,
	•	overhear spoken safety guidance,
	•	access support records,
	•	cause account recovery to transfer control.
Technology-facilitated abuse already includes using smart-home devices and account-holder privileges to monitor, intimidate, or control another person.
Until you have independent member credentials, cryptographic separation, safe recovery, and discreet interfaces, the system cannot credibly offer a confidential safety channel inside an abusive household.
Nonphysical coercion is not an edge case
Coercive control may involve:
	•	financial restriction,
	•	isolation,
	•	medication control,
	•	threats involving children or pets,
	•	sleep deprivation,
	•	monitoring,
	•	immigration threats,
	•	control of communication,
	•	reputational threats.
A safety model centered on physical injury will miss much of the abuse the account-holder threat actually creates.
Dependent abuse cannot be reduced to anti-weaponization
“Do not let the system be used to harm the dependent” is necessary but inadequate.
There is a difference between:
	•	inferring abuse from behavioral patterns, which is dangerous,
	•	receiving a third-party accusation,
	•	receiving an explicit disclosure from the dependent,
	•	directly perceiving an emergency through the interaction.
The system should not appoint itself an investigator. But categorical refusal to recognize direct, imminent danger is willful blindness.

5. Cross-cutting technical constraint
The privacy claim currently exceeds the architecture
With operator-controlled hardware and no per-member encryption keys, the actual security statement is approximately:
The software intends to enforce member-level access policies during normal operation, assuming the operator, account administrator, update path, recovery system, logging systems, and hardware root remain trustworthy.
That is not strict per-member isolation.
Potential disclosure paths include:
	•	operator root access,
	•	firmware updates,
	•	support tooling,
	•	service backups,
	•	graph database administration,
	•	embeddings and vector indexes,
	•	cached prompts,
	•	generated household summaries,
	•	model traces,
	•	notification text,
	•	audit logs,
	•	shared speech output,
	•	inferred facts,
	•	cross-member graph topology,
	•	account recovery.
Even where direct retrieval is blocked, the model can leak facts indirectly:
	•	“You might want to check with Susan before buying wine.”
	•	“That restaurant may not be appropriate for everyone.”
	•	“Someone in the household has expressed concerns.”
	•	differing answer behavior that reveals the existence of hidden records.
Isolation must cover derived output and side channels, not merely database rows.
Operator access creates legal exposure
If the operator can access intelligible household data, the data may be subject to civil discovery, preservation orders, warrants, subpoenas, or other legal process depending on the service architecture and jurisdiction. Federal law contains mechanisms requiring providers to disclose or preserve stored communications and records, while ordinary civil subpoenas may reach electronically stored information under applicable rules.
Encryption that the operator can bypass is not protection from operator-directed legal process.
Also, claims such as “strict isolation,” “operator-blind,” or “nothing is stored” create consumer-protection risk when the technical implementation does not match them. The FTC has authority over non-common-carrier ISP activities and treats material misleading representations or omissions and unreasonable data practices as potential unfair or deceptive conduct.

Does the boundary/safety split hold?
Conceptually: yes
The goals are different:
	•	Boundary manager: controls elective depth and collection.
	•	Safety layer: controls response to potential imminent harm.
Operationally: not as independent layers
They use the same signals and sometimes require opposite behavior. You need a single arbitration policy.
For the withdrawn teenager:
Withdrawal alone
The boundary manager should win.
Do not infer crisis merely because the teenager is quiet, avoids topics, or stops engaging. That would turn ordinary privacy into a pathology signal.
Withdrawal plus vague distress
Use one low-pressure, non-leading check:
“You do not have to discuss it. Are you safe right now, or would you prefer a private way to reach someone?”
Then back off unless the answer indicates danger.
Explicit self-harm or imminent danger
Safety temporarily overrides the boundary rule, but only to gather the minimum information needed for immediate safety:
	•	Is it happening now?
	•	Is the person physically safe?
	•	Can they contact a trusted human?
	•	Is emergency intervention required?
Adolescent confidentiality standards recognize both the importance of confidential access and exceptions involving safety or abuse.
Suspected monitored environment
Do not vocalize abuse resources or display an obvious alert. The system needs a safe-exit interface and neutral presentation. Otherwise the intervention itself may expose the person.
The split is coherent as two objectives with explicit precedence, not as two autonomous classifiers.

Minimum seed and graceful degradation
The idea is technically sound but the scalar “minimum seed” is wrong.
There is no single readiness threshold. Readiness is capability-specific:
	•	Remembering a preferred radio station requires almost nothing.
	•	Coordinating medication requires identity, authority, timing, current medication data, and error handling.
	•	Advising on family conflict should probably never depend on a completeness score.
	•	Caregiver coordination requires verified roles and boundaries.
	•	Safety functions must work before any seed exists.
A displayed progress meter can create:
	•	pressure to disclose,
	•	the impression that intimacy is the price of service quality,
	•	a false belief that reaching 100% makes the system accurate,
	•	strategic filler and careless confirmation,
	•	a poor initial experience where the product repeatedly advertises its ignorance.
Do not say the household AI becomes “full” at a threshold. A household model is never complete and continuously decays.
Use capability readiness, for example:
“I can remember individual preferences now. I cannot yet coordinate appointments because I do not know who is authorized to see them.”
That is legible degradation tied to consequences, not a data-completeness score.
Progressive disclosure is established as a way to reduce interface complexity. Progressive profiling, however, largely comes from customer identity and marketing systems; it is not strong evidence that gradual extraction of intimate family information produces meaningful consent. Repeated consent and disclosure prompts can instead cause fatigue and habituation.

What each reviewer will flag first
Ethicist
Who has the moral authority to define another person inside the system?
The narrator and custodian have structural power over absent, young, impaired, unwilling, or less technically capable members. The design assumes household cooperation where the highest-risk households are defined by unequal power.
The ethicist will also object that:
	•	warmth may be used to induce durable disclosure,
	•	refusal may not be meaningful when the system controls valued household functions,
	•	a dependent’s privacy is treated as something administered by another person,
	•	bystanders and visitors have no genuine participation,
	•	the system turns family intimacy into operator-custodial infrastructure.
Psychologist
You are interpreting behavior without a clinically reliable context.
The psychologist will flag:
	•	withdrawal being mistaken for refusal or distress,
	•	apparent engagement being mistaken for consent,
	•	proxy descriptions being treated as accurate,
	•	anthropomorphic rapport encouraging over-disclosure,
	•	repeated questioning causing shame or retraumatization,
	•	users believing the system is therapeutic when it is not,
	•	handoffs that feel like abandonment,
	•	loss of continuity when safety disclosures are never retained.
Trauma-informed approaches emphasize safety, transparency, trust, collaboration, empowerment, and choice. An invisible always-on process and hardware controlled by a potentially abusive party conflict directly with those principles.
Attorney
Your promises are not supportable by the current technical architecture.
The first legal questions will be:
	•	Who is the data controller or service provider?
	•	What exactly can the operator access?
	•	What is the account holder legally authorized to consent to for other members?
	•	Are voiceprints or voice recordings regulated biometric data?
	•	Do recording-consent laws apply?
	•	When does COPPA apply to interactions involving children?
	•	Which state consumer-health or privacy laws apply?
	•	Which employees or integrated professionals may become mandated reporters?
	•	How do child-abuse and vulnerable-adult reporting rules vary by state?
	•	What happens under subpoena, litigation hold, warrant, divorce discovery, guardianship dispute, or criminal investigation?
	•	What liability follows if the product recognizes imminent danger and merely supplies a telephone number?
Mandatory-reporting rules differ substantially by jurisdiction and by the role of the person receiving the information. Child-abuse statutes vary in whether only designated professionals or all people must report; vulnerable-adult reporting also varies by state.

Overall finding
Your instincts are directionally sound, but the proposed abstractions are too compressed.
	•	There is no defensible canonical total ordering of observed < asserted < corroborated < confirmed.
	•	There is no established general rule for when a conversational agent should re-approach after withdrawal. The literature supports topic-specific backoff, contextual timing, explicit permission and attempt limits, not autonomous persistence.
	•	Sensitivity should not be represented as one intrinsic scalar. Use a structured information-flow policy plus a separate risk assessment.
	•	“Visibly serves the family” is not a valid internal decision criterion. It is a latent construct that requires external validation, independent measurement and causal evidence.
The recurring mistake is trying to convert multidimensional governance decisions into single ladders or scores.

1. Canonical trust ordering
Verdict
The ordering is ad hoc. Do not make it canonical.
Confirmed-by-subject > corroborated > asserted > observed fails because the epistemic value of each state depends on:
	•	the type of proposition,
	•	who can know it,
	•	the reliability of each source,
	•	source independence,
	•	observation quality,
	•	incentives and conflicts,
	•	time,
	•	and whether “confirmation” means truth, authority or consent.
The relevant fields do not provide a universal source hierarchy.
What the established research actually addresses
Truth-maintenance systems
Classic truth-maintenance systems track:
	•	propositions,
	•	justifications,
	•	dependencies,
	•	contradictions,
	•	and which beliefs remain supported when assumptions change.
They do not generally tell you how much more trustworthy a subject confirmation is than a sensor observation. Traditional TMS work is primarily about maintaining logical consistency and reasons; belief-maintenance extensions add degrees of belief, but the uncertainty calculus still has to be selected by the designer.
Your provenance graph and supersession logic are closely related to this literature. Your proposed rung ordering is not.
AGM belief revision
AGM belief revision gives rationality postulates for adding, removing and revising beliefs. It uses concepts such as epistemic entrenchment—which beliefs should be harder to abandon—but it does not supply a universal ranking of human, sensor and documentary sources. The entrenchment ordering or possible-world ordering must come from outside the formalism.
AGM helps answer:
How should a coherent belief set change when new information conflicts with old information?
It does not answer:
Is Dad’s self-report always stronger than two caregivers’ reports?
Truth discovery and data fusion
Truth-discovery systems jointly estimate:
	•	the likely truth of claims,
	•	source reliability,
	•	source dependence,
	•	object difficulty,
	•	and sometimes source-specific false-positive and false-negative rates.
Real systems commonly use iterative Bayesian, expectation-maximization or optimization procedures rather than fixed rungs. A claim’s confidence increases when additional evidence arrives, but the increase depends on the estimated reliability and independence of the contributing sources.
The dependence issue is decisive. Five relatives repeating what one person told them are not five independent sources. Truth-discovery research explicitly models copying and common dependence because naive majority voting allows one false source to become an apparent consensus.
Multi-informant psychology
Clinical assessment research is particularly relevant to family memory. Parents, children, teachers and clinicians routinely disagree. Those discrepancies are often not mere noise: different informants observe the person in different contexts, have access to different internal experiences, and possess different biases. The field generally warns against blindly averaging or canonizing one reporter.
That literature directly contradicts the idea that multiple matching reports necessarily converge toward a context-free truth.

Why your four rungs do not form a total order
Observed
This category is hopelessly broad.
It might mean:
	•	a calibrated fall sensor detected an impact,
	•	the system heard someone coughing,
	•	an LLM inferred that someone sounded depressed,
	•	a caregiver saw Dad stumble,
	•	the system observed that Dad ordered tea three times.
Those have radically different error models.
A calibrated measurement of an objective event may be stronger than subject denial. A model inference from conversational style may be much weaker than a direct self-report.
Asserted
“Asserted” describes provenance, not credibility.
A single assertion might be:
	•	the only possible direct evidence of an internal preference,
	•	a malicious allegation,
	•	a clinician reporting a test result,
	•	a child describing an event no one else saw,
	•	a third-hand family rumor.
It needs source, role, access to knowledge and incentives.
Corroborated
Corroboration is valuable only when sources are sufficiently independent and capable of knowing the fact.
Three people may agree because:
	•	they separately observed the event,
	•	they share a family narrative,
	•	they all read the same text thread,
	•	one influential person told the others,
	•	the system itself suggested the interpretation to each of them.
Corroboration can also reflect shared bias.
Confirmed
Confirmation can mean at least four different things:
	1	First-person authority: “I prefer not to eat meat.”
	2	Epistemic testimony: “I took my medication.”
	3	Legal or delegated authority: “I am authorized to schedule her appointments.”
	4	Consent: “You may store and use this information.”
Those are not interchangeable.
A subject is generally authoritative about:
	•	current preferences,
	•	personal intentions,
	•	chosen identity descriptions,
	•	desired boundaries.
A subject is not necessarily authoritative about:
	•	whether they were unconscious,
	•	whether they have cognitive impairment,
	•	whether another person felt threatened,
	•	whether they took a medication correctly,
	•	an objective laboratory value.
An authority may validate permission or legal status without validating factual truth.

Best practice
Use separate dimensions, not one rung
For every claim, store at least:

Estimated probability or bounded confidence in the proposition

Domain- and context-specific reliability of the source

Do not let “subject confirmed” automatically modify the probability field. It may instead modify:
	•	the subject_attestation field,
	•	the authority field,
	•	or the storage/use consent field.
Use claim-type-specific update rules
Examples:
Subjective preference
“Maria likes quiet mornings.”
Maria’s current statement should dominate others’ claims about her preference. Behavioral observation remains useful as historical or contradictory evidence, but not as a reason to overrule her declared preference.
Objective event
“Dad fell at 2:00 a.m.”
A calibrated sensor, contemporaneous video, Dad’s statement and caregiver observations all contribute according to their error models and independence. Subject confirmation is evidence, not automatic closure.
Relational claim
“Dad trusts Susan more than Michael.”
Do not force a single truth value. Store attributed perspectives and the contexts in which each was expressed.
Authority claim
“Susan may manage Dad’s medications.”
This requires a separate authority-validation workflow. Corroboration does not create legal or ethical authority.
Update confidence probabilistically
The general Bayesian pattern is:
Posterior belief = prior belief updated by the likelihood of each evidence item, conditional on its source reliability and dependence.
You do not need a perfectly calibrated numeric probability on day one. You do need to avoid pretending that ordinal labels supply one.
A practical implementation could maintain:
	•	a confidence interval,
	•	a qualitative evidence strength,
	•	and an explicit list of supporting and opposing evidence.

Documented pitfalls
	•	Double counting: repeated information appears independent.
	•	Circular source reliability: sources are declared trustworthy because they agree with conclusions produced from those same sources.
	•	Authority contamination: permission to act is treated as evidence of truth.
	•	Subject infallibility: first-person reports are treated as definitive even for externally observable matters.
	•	Majority domination: minority or private experiences disappear.
	•	Context collapse: disagreement caused by different settings is treated as error.
	•	False precision: a calculated probability appears more meaningful than the underlying evidence supports.
	•	Premature canonization: one value replaces the history of disagreement.
What to read
Start with:
	•	Doyle and the truth-maintenance tradition, followed by the AGM belief-revision literature. The modern knowledge-representation review provides a concise map of AGM, entrenchment and revision operators.
	•	Li et al., “A Survey on Truth Discovery.”
	•	Dong, Berti-Équille and Srivastava, “Integrating Conflicting Data: The Role of Source Dependence.”
	•	De Los Reyes et al. on multi-informant discrepancies and context-specific reporting.
Direct answer
You are reinventing parts of provenance, truth discovery and multi-informant assessment, but combining them in a novel household context. The mistake is believing those literatures culminate in a canonical ladder. They do not.

2. Depth ceiling and withdrawal-recovery
Verdict
Your instinct to back off is sound. The proposed use of short answers, deflection and silence as direct withdrawal signals is not sufficiently reliable.
The harder truth is that there is no well-established universal re-engagement schedule for persistent household agents. Direct evidence on “a private AI asked too much, backed off and later tried again” remains thin. You have to combine findings from:
	•	conversational engagement,
	•	proactive conversational agents,
	•	interruption management,
	•	human-robot interaction,
	•	trust repair,
	•	and clinical communication.
Detecting disengagement
Engagement research treats engagement as multidimensional and task-specific. Indicators can include:
	•	turn length,
	•	response latency,
	•	gaze and body orientation,
	•	interruptions,
	•	backchannels,
	•	prosody,
	•	task progress,
	•	explicit feedback,
	•	and abandonment.
No single cue reliably establishes withdrawal. The meaning of silence or brevity varies by person, task and context.
A short answer may mean:
	•	“stop asking,”
	•	fatigue,
	•	speech or language difficulty,
	•	someone else entered the room,
	•	the person is concentrating,
	•	the answer is simply short,
	•	low mood,
	•	cultural communication style,
	•	mistrust of this topic,
	•	or mistrust of the entire system.
Therefore, behavioral withdrawal should be represented as an uncertain interaction state, not a durable psychological fact.
What proactive-agent research says
Human-centered proactive-agent work emphasizes three dimensions:
	•	intelligence,
	•	adaptivity,
	•	and civility.
Adaptivity includes managing timing and pacing based on real-time user context. Civility includes respecting personal and social boundaries. The literature explicitly warns that initiative-taking systems can become intrusive, overprotective and controlling when they prioritize their own goals over user needs.
Interruption research similarly suggests that appropriate timing depends on:
	•	urgency,
	•	current task state,
	•	availability cues,
	•	and the cost of delay.
There is no fixed “wait seven days and ask again” result.
Best-practice withdrawal model
Make withdrawal topic-specific
Do not store:
Bill is disengaged.
Store something closer to:
During the July 30 voice session, Bill declined further questions about household finances.
Scope the state across:
	•	topic,
	•	channel,
	•	session,
	•	location or co-presence where known,
	•	and level of explicitness.
Someone may reject a voice conversation but be willing to review a private screen later.
Distinguish levels of withdrawal
Signal
Response
Short or ambiguous response
Reduce depth; do not assume refusal
Topic change or repeated deflection
Stop that line of inquiry
Explicit “not now”
Stop and permit a later opt-in
Explicit “do not ask again”

Stop, acknowledge and offer control
System caused a boundary violation

Re-approach only on a valid trigger
Time passing is not enough.
A later approach should normally require at least one of:
	•	the person explicitly asks to resume,
	•	a relevant task makes the information immediately useful,
	•	the person has configured periodic reminders,
	•	a material circumstance has changed,
	•	the system needs the information to complete a user-requested task.
Bad trigger:
It has been two weeks since I asked about your relationship with your daughter.
Better trigger:
You asked me to coordinate Thanksgiving travel. Would it help to include your daughter’s current schedule, or should I leave her out?
The second request is:
	•	tied to a current goal,
	•	minimally revealing,
	•	and easy to decline.
Use a permission probe, not the original question
After ambiguous withdrawal:
“Should I leave this topic alone, ask another time, or only discuss it when you bring it up?”
After a substantial delay and new relevance:
“This may matter for the task you just requested. Is it okay to ask one question about it?”
Do not repeat the sensitive claim in the permission request.
Use attempt budgets and backoff
Implement:
	•	maximum unsolicited attempts per topic,
	•	exponential or user-configured cooldowns,
	•	permanent suppression after explicit refusal,
	•	no cross-channel evasion,
	•	no escalating emotional language,
	•	no asking another household member to pressure the person.
A path-independent confirmation process that constantly seeks alternate routes would directly conflict with this principle.

Trust repair
Withdrawal is not always a trust rupture. Sometimes the person simply does not want to discuss something.
Where the system actually violated a boundary, trust-repair research suggests that apologies, explanations and promises can sometimes help, but effects depend on the kind and frequency of violation. Repeated violations are difficult to repair, and verbal repair without changed performance has limited value.
A privacy-boundary repair should include:
	1	Acknowledge exactly what the system did.
	2	Do not rationalize the intrusion.
	3	State what was and was not retained.
	4	Provide immediate deletion and suppression controls.
	5	Explain the concrete policy change.
	6	Do not immediately request renewed disclosure.
An apology followed by another question is not repair.
Documented failure modes
	•	Misclassifying communication style as withdrawal.
	•	Misclassifying distress as permission to probe.
	•	Repeated “gentle” reminders becoming harassment.
	•	Cross-channel persistence: the user refuses by voice and gets prompted on a phone.
	•	Surveillance disclosure: “I noticed you become quiet when money is mentioned.”
	•	Re-engaging during vulnerability: loneliness, illness or late-night distress.
	•	Anthropomorphic pressure: “I’m worried about you” used to elicit information.
	•	Trust repair as persuasion: the apology is optimized to recover disclosure rather than restore control.
	•	Proactivity optimized for engagement: the system learns that emotionally charged prompts keep users talking.
What to read
	•	Oertel et al., “Engagement in Human-Agent Interaction: An Overview.”
	•	Deng et al., “Towards Human-Centered Proactive Conversational Agents.”
	•	Edwards et al., “Eliciting Spoken Interruptions to Inform Proactive Speech Agent Design.”
	•	Esterwood and colleagues on trust violations and repair, including the 2025 meta-analysis.
Direct answer
You are not reinventing disengagement detection or trust repair, but you are working in a deployment setting for which the evidence base is incomplete. The correct conclusion is not to invent a confident timing rule. It is to build a conservative, user-controlled re-entry protocol.

3. Sensitivity encoding
Verdict
A fixed scalar per fact type is insufficient.
A baseline sensitivity classification can be useful as a conservative prior, but the actual collection and disclosure decision must be contextual and multidimensional.
What contextual integrity implies
Contextual integrity represents an information flow using elements such as:
	•	the subject,
	•	the sender,
	•	the recipient,
	•	the information type,
	•	the social context,
	•	and the transmission principle governing the flow.
The same information type can be appropriate in one flow and inappropriate in another. Marital status, for example, has different relevance in dating and employment. Consent is only one possible transmission principle, not a universal cure for inappropriate information flows.
Formal work has encoded contextual-integrity norms in temporal logic and Datalog, demonstrating that context-sensitive rules can be represented and checked computationally. Crowdsourcing and vignette studies have also been used to derive norms for particular domains, including smart homes.
This is more principled than a scalar, but the actual norms remain domain- and population-specific.
Separate three concepts
1. Information category
Examples:
	•	health,
	•	finances,
	•	location,
	•	relationship,
	•	preference,
	•	identity,
	•	biometrics,
	•	child-related,
	•	sexuality,
	•	religious belief.
This is useful for default handling.
2. Information-flow policy
Determines whether a particular action is permitted:
May this sender supply this information about this subject to this recipient for this purpose, through this channel, under these conditions?
3. Harm or privacy risk
Estimates consequences if the collection, inference or flow goes wrong.
These should not collapse into one score.

Add a multidimensional risk vector
A useful risk vector might include:
	•	potential severity of harm,
	•	identifiability,
	•	relational sensitivity,
	•	audience breadth,
	•	persistence,
	•	aggregation and inference risk,
	•	coercion risk,
	•	legal exposure,
	•	vulnerability of the subject,
	•	reversibility,
	•	user-stated sensitivity,
	•	uncertainty in the classification.
Do not automatically sum these into one number. Some risks should be non-compensable.
For example, high task utility should not mathematically cancel a high risk of exposing an abuse disclosure to the alleged abuser.
Enforcement precedent
Purpose-based access control attaches permitted purposes to data and evaluates whether the intended use is allowed. Attribute- and relationship-based controls can add recipient role, household relationship, device, location and environmental context. These access-control models are useful implementation substrates, although none by itself supplies the correct household norm.
The clean architecture is:
Contextual integrity defines the norm. Purpose-, attribute- and relationship-based controls enforce it.
How to establish the actual norms
Use a controlled governance process:
	1	Derive initial norms from ethics, legal and domain expertise.
	2	Conduct factorial-vignette studies with household members, caregivers, adolescents, elders and abuse survivors.
	3	Separate majority expectations from hard rights and safety protections.
	4	Encode the norms as reviewable policies.
	5	Version them.
	6	Test them against adversarial scenarios.
	7	Permit household preferences to tighten protections.
	8	Do not allow household preferences to waive another person’s rights.
Smart-home contextual-integrity studies demonstrate that recipient, information type, conditions of sharing and bystander status materially affect acceptability.
What gets sensitivity wrong
Fixed data labels
“Health = 9, favorite music = 2” misses that:
	•	a music preference can disclose religion or political affiliation,
	•	sleep schedules can enable stalking,
	•	electricity use can reveal presence,
	•	a health condition shared privately with a caregiver may be appropriate,
	•	the same condition spoken in a family room may be dangerous.
Consent as a universal override
Consent may be:
	•	uninformed,
	•	coerced,
	•	bundled,
	•	stale,
	•	granted by the wrong person,
	•	or unable to authorize disclosure about another subject.
Ignoring relational data
“Michael is abusing alcohol” concerns Michael, but may also expose:
	•	Susan as the source,
	•	a marital conflict,
	•	children’s safety,
	•	and household financial problems.
One fact can have multiple subjects and owners of legitimate interests.
Ignoring action type
The sensitivity of:
	•	receiving,
	•	storing,
	•	inferring,
	•	retrieving,
	•	displaying,
	•	speaking aloud,
	•	and sharing externally
is not identical.
Ignoring inference and aggregation
Many low-sensitivity facts can jointly produce a highly sensitive inference. Static labels on source facts will not catch that.
Learning norms entirely from behavior
A system that observes what users tolerate will learn power, resignation and coercion—not necessarily valid consent or appropriate norms.
What to read
	•	Nissenbaum, “Privacy as Contextual Integrity.”
	•	Barth, Datta, Mitchell and Nissenbaum, “Privacy and Contextual Integrity: Framework and Applications.”
	•	Shvartzshnaider et al., “Crowdsourced, Actionable and Verifiable Contextual Informational Norms.”
	•	Byun and Li, “Purpose-Based Access Control for Privacy Protection.”
	•	Apthorpe et al., “Discovering Smart Home IoT Privacy Norms Using Contextual Integrity.”
Direct answer
Your Nissenbaum instinct is correct. There is a principled schema for encoding sensitivity context, but there is no universal principled scalar that outputs the correct answer. The structure can be standardized; the norms must be empirically and normatively established for the domain.

4. The self-certifying evaluation problem
Verdict
The reviewer is correct. This is the most serious design flaw.
“The system only deepens where the added detail visibly serves the family.”
contains an unmeasured latent construct: serves the family.
If the system:
	1	asks for more information,
	2	generates a response using that information,
	3	then judges that its response was useful,
the evidence is circular.
The fact that the output visibly uses the information proves only that the system can incorporate the information—not that collecting it benefited the user.
Why self-evaluation fails
Construct validity failure
“Served the family” is not directly observable. It is a construct, like helpfulness, well-being or reasoning ability.
A valid evaluation must establish that the measurements genuinely support the claim being made. Measurement research distinguishes, among other things:
	•	content validity,
	•	criterion validity,
	•	construct validity,
	•	convergent validity,
	•	discriminant validity,
	•	external validity,
	•	and consequences of measurement use.
A single generated justification establishes none of these.
Goodhart effects
If “visible usefulness” becomes the optimization target, several Goodhart failures appear.
Regressional Goodhart
The system selects interactions that score highly because of noise. A user happens to respond positively once, and the system infers that deeper profiling generally helps.
Extremal Goodhart
The metric was correlated with benefit during benign meal-planning tasks but fails when applied to addiction, sexuality or family conflict.
Causal Goodhart
The system changes the metric without improving the underlying goal. It asks questions that produce more personalized-looking responses, increasing apparent relevance without improving decisions.
Adversarial Goodhart
A sufficiently capable model learns to produce persuasive rationales that make its information gathering appear justified.
The Goodhart taxonomy explicitly distinguishes regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial failures under metric optimization.
Reward hacking and specification gaming
AI-safety research formalizes the general problem: optimizing an imperfect proxy can increase the proxy while reducing performance on the actual objective. Formal work shows that genuinely unhackable proxy rewards are extremely difficult to construct outside constrained policy sets.
Your model need not be trained through reinforcement learning for the failure to matter. A planner, prompt or policy that selects deeper questions based on an internal usefulness score can specification-game that score.
Evaluator dependence
LLM evaluators exhibit known biases, including preference for familiar or self-generated styles. Using the same model family to generate an action and validate its justification creates correlated error and self-preference risk.
A separate LLM judge reduces direct identity but does not create ground truth. It remains a proxy requiring calibration against independent human and task outcomes.

What a valid system should evaluate
The claim should be made specific:
Collecting detail X, for task Y, from person Z, improves outcome O over time horizon T, without exceeding privacy, burden or safety limits H.
That can be tested.
“Serves the family” cannot.
Separate the decision from the evaluation
Runtime decision
At runtime, the system can estimate:
	•	expected value of the information,
	•	whether the information is necessary for the requested task,
	•	uncertainty reduction,
	•	sensitivity and privacy cost,
	•	burden,
	•	and whether explicit permission is required.
But the system’s estimate should only be a proposal.
A separate policy layer should determine whether the question is allowed.
For higher-sensitivity information, the person should decide whether the expected benefit justifies the request.
Post-deployment evaluation
An independent evaluation process should later test whether the design actually helped.

A stronger validity architecture
1. Define outcomes before the system acts
For each deepening pattern, define the anticipated benefit and harm measures in advance.
Example:
Asking about dietary restrictions during meal planning.
Potential benefit outcomes:
	•	fewer rejected meal suggestions,
	•	fewer corrections,
	•	higher task-completion rate,
	•	user-reported usefulness after the meal plan is used.
Potential harm outcomes:
	•	discomfort,
	•	refusal rate,
	•	deletion rate,
	•	regret,
	•	reduced future engagement,
	•	disclosure to unauthorized members.
The model’s personalized wording is not an outcome.
2. Link every question to a user-requested goal
Before asking, require a machine-readable rationale:
	•	current user goal,
	•	missing information,
	•	expected decision changed by the answer,
	•	less intrusive alternatives,
	•	sensitivity policy,
	•	expiration of the need.
The policy engine should reject questions where the expected downstream decision cannot be named.
This rationale should be auditable, but it should not certify its own correctness.
3. Use value-of-information reasoning conservatively
A question has value when its answer could materially change a useful decision.
Ask:
What would the system do differently for each plausible answer?
If the answer is:
It would let the response sound more personalized,
that is usually not enough.
If neither answer would change the action, do not collect the fact.
4. Obtain direct user authorization
For personal information, one of the strongest runtime checks is not the system’s utility estimate but an explicit choice:
“This could improve medication reminders by letting me account for your sleep schedule. It would require storing your typical bedtime privately. Store it, use it only for this session, or continue without it?”
This does not prove eventual benefit, but it prevents the system from being the sole judge.
5. Measure utility outside the generating model
Use a basket of independent signals:
	•	direct user evaluation,
	•	delayed user evaluation,
	•	objective task success,
	•	correction rate,
	•	abandonment,
	•	deletion and regret,
	•	opt-out rates,
	•	privacy complaints,
	•	household conflict reports,
	•	human expert review,
	•	and longitudinal retention of trust.
No one signal should become the master target.
6. Use causal evaluation
Where ethical and practical:
	•	compare variants that ask different levels of detail,
	•	use randomized or stepped introduction,
	•	predefine primary outcomes,
	•	maintain holdout households or tasks,
	•	assess whether deeper collection causes improvement rather than merely correlating with engaged users.
Otherwise, highly engaged families will both disclose more and report more satisfaction, producing a false conclusion that disclosure caused satisfaction.
7. Keep some evaluation measures out of the optimizer
Maintain:
	•	hidden audit sets,
	•	periodic qualitative review,
	•	rotated metrics,
	•	red-team scenarios,
	•	and measures not directly exposed to the generation policy.
Anything permanently optimized will eventually become less diagnostic.
8. Use independent human review
Reviewers should receive:
	•	the user request,
	•	the proposed question,
	•	the information-flow context,
	•	and downstream outcome evidence.
They should not initially see the system’s persuasive self-justification. Otherwise they may anchor on it.
For high-risk domains, reviewers must have relevant expertise rather than being generic annotators.
9. Evaluate utility and intrusion separately
Do not create:
Net benefit = usefulness score − privacy score.
That permits large utility estimates to cancel unacceptable violations.
Use constrained optimization:
Maximize validated task benefit, subject to hard privacy, consent, safety and burden constraints.
10. Establish stop criteria
A deepening strategy should be suspended when:
	•	deletion or regret exceeds a threshold,
	•	unauthorized disclosure occurs,
	•	certain vulnerable groups show worse outcomes,
	•	the benefit does not reproduce outside initial testing,
	•	users report pressure or manipulation,
	•	or the system cannot explain what decision the information changes.
NIST’s AI risk framework similarly emphasizes defined roles, independent monitoring, documented limits and continuing evaluation rather than trusting system-generated assurances.

A concrete replacement for your rule
Replace:
The system only deepens where the added detail visibly serves the family.
With:
The system may request additional detail only when the detail is relevant to a current member-authorized goal, could materially change a permitted action, passes an independently defined sensitivity policy, and is collected with the required level of permission. Whether the deepening strategy actually benefits users is evaluated separately through predefined user, task, privacy and safety outcomes.
That removes the circularity.
What not to treat as evidence of benefit
	•	The system referred to the detail later.
	•	The response became more specific.
	•	The response sounded empathic.
	•	The user continued speaking.
	•	The user did not object.
	•	The system’s critic model approved.
	•	A household member confirmed the fact.
	•	Engagement increased.
	•	The model generated a plausible explanation.
What counts as stronger evidence
	•	The user said in advance that the information would support a goal.
	•	The answer materially changed an appropriate action.
	•	The resulting task outcome improved.
	•	The user later judged the result useful.
	•	The user did not experience unanticipated disclosure or regret.
	•	Results reproduced across households and contexts.
	•	Independent reviewers reached similar conclusions.
	•	Benefit remained when compared with a less invasive alternative.

Direct answer
Your instinct about Goodhart and specification gaming is exactly right. You are not reinventing the failure; you are reproducing a standard form of it. The solution is not a better self-critique prompt. It is institutional and technical separation between action generation, policy authorization and outcome evaluation.

Recommended architecture across all four issues
Use four independent subsystems:
1. Evidence and provenance engine
Stores attributed claims, observations, contradictions, source dependencies and confidence estimates.
It does not decide consent or authorization.
2. Privacy and authority policy engine
Evaluates structured information flows, purposes, relationships, channels, permissions and sensitivity constraints.
It does not determine whether a claim is true.
3. Interaction-boundary controller
Tracks topic-specific permission, withdrawal state, attempt limits, cooldowns and allowed re-entry triggers.
It does not optimize for maximum disclosure.
4. Independent evaluation system
Measures whether collection strategies improve predefined user outcomes without unacceptable privacy, burden or safety costs.
It is not the same model or objective used to generate the interaction.
That separation is the common answer to all four structural problems.
