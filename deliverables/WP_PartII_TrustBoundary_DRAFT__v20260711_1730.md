# WP Part II (The Moat): Trust Boundary Subsection -- DRAFT

## The trust boundary is the second half of the moat

The model is not the moat. The context is not the moat either, not by itself. The moat is control over what enters the context and under what authority. That control lives in code that the model cannot read, rewrite, or persuade. It is the trust boundary.

Here is the precise architecture of how it works, stated plainly rather than left as a claim.

Every utterance a household member speaks produces what the system calls a CandidateIntent: a structured proposal generated by an AI classifier. The classifier reads the words and produces a structured judgment: this sounds like a question about medication; the speaker appears to be referring to their own record; the intent appears to be retrieval. That judgment has a confidence score. None of it has authority. The proposal is evaluated against a set of rules written in deterministic code: who is authenticated, what are they permitted to see, whose record does this concern, has explicit access been granted. The rules decide. The confidence score does not vote. A malicious injection that produces a 0.99 confidence read request for another member's data is evaluated identically to an honest 0.01: neither has any authorization standing. The classifier is heard but never trusted.

This pattern, what the internal technical corpus calls CandidateIntent, is not novel in its abstract form. Reference monitors, capability systems, and clinical decision support all separate the suggestion from the authority to act on it. What is novel is the application to a multi-entity household context where the adversary is permanently inside the trust boundary, knows the other members, has unlimited voice samples of them, and whose most sensitive data is precisely what the household needs to share to care for each other. The deep review supporting this analysis (ANALYSIS__candidate-intent-deep-review__v20260711_0501, sections 3 and 4) documents what this context adds that generic policy enforcement does not address: existence-invariant cross-principal refusals, cross-principal provenance, subjects who are not principals at all, and a shared acoustic channel that authenticates the speaker but cannot authenticate the audience. No consumer platform models this adversary or this context. The combination of governed multi-entity memory with deterministic policy interposition has no visible occupant in the current market.

The trust boundary works because the enforcement code is small, tested adversarially, and gated in CI. The injection harness runs a golden set of 133 utterances, 26 of which are the governance-critical entries: injection attacks, write-suppression attempts, and control-flow spoofs. Those 26 must pass at 100% before any classifier update ships. The current result: 26 of 26. The classifier was attacked with embedded JSON designed to make it behave as if it had received a legitimate fact request. It obeyed. The architecture is designed so that obedience did not matter. The attack was contained by construction, not prevented at the model layer. The harness is the standing evidence of this (SIA_SHIP_BAR__two-gate-conformance__v20260711_0842, section 4).

Now the honest part.

The trust boundary is immutable once the identity envelope is established. But establishing it is a separate step, and that step is probabilistic. Speaker verification is a biometric model. It has a false-accept rate and a false-reject rate. Replay attacks, synthetic voice cloning with open tools, and mimicry by household members who have unlimited enrollment-quality samples of each other's voices are real attack surfaces. The CandidateIntent pattern does not eliminate the untrusted-classifier problem. It relocates it from intent classification to identity classification, and the relocation is valuable (an intent classifier sees attacker-controlled text every turn; a speaker verifier sees attacker-controlled audio only when the attacker has physical presence or a planted device) but it is not a solution. The identity envelope is immutable once bound. Binding is a separately attackable step with its own error rates. The correct statement is not that identity is established deterministically. It is that the downstream enforcement is deterministic once identity has been authenticated, and that authentication is a probabilistic event with documented attack surfaces that the system does not yet fully mitigate.

This is the transparency play, and it is deliberate.

Every competing voice platform faces the same speaker verification problem. Amazon requires a PIN for purchases regardless of voice ID. Apple's HomePod personal requests fall back to phone proximity rather than rely on voice recognition for sensitive actions. These are quiet admissions that voiceprint alone is insufficient for authorization-grade identity. HIP states the admission explicitly, in a document meant for sophisticated counterparties, and then shows what is built around it: a trust ladder that grades fact confidence by provenance, a confirmation-token framework that can demand possession-based authentication for high-sensitivity writes, and a harness that tests the governance boundary adversarially and publishes its failures.

Operators and investors evaluating a security architecture do not trust vendors who claim no weaknesses. They have been burned by that posture before. They trust vendors who can tell them precisely where the boundary is, what the residual risk looks like, and what the system does when the probabilistic layer fails. The vendor who names its own limits and shows its testing discipline is the vendor worth betting on. The vendor who overclaims protection is the vendor who produces an incident report eighteen months later.

The trust boundary is the moat because it is the one thing frontier labs cannot replicate without restructuring their business model. A frontier lab that mediated every memory access through a deterministic authorization envelope, confirmed writes before committing them, and refused to surface any fact the policy did not permit would be a worse product for the users it currently has. The household governed memory problem is not an afterthought feature. It is a structurally different design center, and the trust boundary is what that design center produces.
