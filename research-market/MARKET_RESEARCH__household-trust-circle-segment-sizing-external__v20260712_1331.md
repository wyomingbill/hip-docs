# Headline finding

**The addressable U.S. segment is large, but not as large as "all households."**

Best defensible estimate:

* **Core near-term segment:** **45%-55% of U.S. households**, roughly **60-73 million households**
* **Broader plausible segment:** **55%-65%**, roughly **73-86 million households**
* **Strongest initial use case (older adult/caregiver governance):** approximately **15%-25% of households**, or **20-33 million households**
* **Truly single-principal households with no meaningful outside involvement:** probably only **15%-25% of households**, but this is an estimate; no national survey directly measures this category.

The central market insight is not merely that households are small. It is that:

> **About 63%-64% of U.S. households contain only one or two residents, and many one-person households still function as two-party information systems because an adult child, spouse living elsewhere, relative, or caregiver participates in health, financial, or household management.**

The segment is therefore **mass-market in demographic scale**, but the immediately urgent, payable segment is narrower and concentrated in aging, caregiving, chronic illness, disability, and household financial coordination.

# 1. U.S. household-size distribution

The United States had approximately **132.7 million households in the 2024 American Community Survey**. Census separately reported **38.5 million one-person households in 2024, equal to 29% of all households**. [1]

The best current rounded distribution:

| Household size   | Share of U.S. households | Approximate households |
| ---------------- | -----------------------: | ---------------------: |
| 1 person         |                  **29%** |       **38.5 million** |
| 2 people         |              **34%-35%** |      **45-46 million** |
| 3 people         |            **about 15%** |         **20 million** |
| 4 people         |        **about 12%-13%** |      **16-17 million** |
| 3-4 combined     |              **27%-28%** |      **36-37 million** |
| 5 or more        |          **about 8%-9%** |      **11-12 million** |
| **1-2 combined** |              **63%-64%** |      **84-85 million** |

The one-person number is directly reported by Census. The remaining rounded percentages are derived from the 2024 ACS household-size tables and should be treated as **approximately plus or minus 1 percentage point**, rather than false precision. The historical Census series confirms the long-term movement toward one- and two-person households. [2]

## International comparison

The U.S. is not unusual among developed economies. Several European and Asian markets have an even stronger small-household profile:

* The EU had about **203 million households in 2025**, including more than **76 million single-adult households without children**, roughly **37%-38%** of households. [3]
* Average household size is below 2.3 in many Northern and Western European countries and Japan. OECD figures range from approximately **1.7-1.8 people per household in Lithuania and Estonia** to above 3.5 in some Latin American OECD countries. [4]
* Japan and Korea have historically had one-person household shares around one-third or higher. [5]

**Conclusion:** A product optimized for one- and two-person trust circles is not merely a U.S. phenomenon. It may be even better aligned with Northern Europe, Japan, South Korea, and parts of Western Europe.

# 2. Dyad segments

These categories overlap heavily. They must **not** be added together. For example, an older married couple may also have an involved adult child and a paid caregiver.

## A. Married or cohabiting couples without children in the household

### Best estimate

* **Strict two-adult couple households:** **38-43 million**
* **Share of all U.S. households:** **29%-32%**

Census reported about **36 million married-couple households without their own children under 18** in its recent family-household analysis. [6]

That figure is not identical to "exactly two adults":

* Some have adult children living in the household.
* Some contain other relatives.
* "No own children under 18" does not mean no children of any age.
* It excludes unmarried cohabiting couples.

Adding childless cohabiting couples produces a broader estimate of roughly **42-46 million couple households**, or **32%-35% of all households**. However, the cleaner target (two adults and no other resident) is probably closer to **38-43 million**.

### Product relevance

This is the largest clean dyad category. Governance may involve:

* private individual context;
* shared household context;
* financial information;
* health information;
* emergency authority;
* separate versus jointly owned facts.

However, ordinary healthy couples may not perceive an urgent problem. The market is large, but willingness to pay is likely driven by age, assets, health complexity, or administrative burden.

---

## B. Couple plus one adult child

### Best estimate

* **6-10 million households**
* **5%-8% of U.S. households**

There is no single Census table that cleanly isolates "a couple plus exactly one adult child." This estimate is constructed from:

* adult children living with parents;
* three-person family-household distributions;
* multigenerational-household counts;
* Census data showing substantial numbers of young adults remain in parental homes.

Census counted approximately **6 million multigenerational households in 2020**, representing **4.7% of all households**, but its definition generally requires three generations or certain grandparent configurations. It therefore misses many parent-plus-adult-child households. [7]

Census also found **25.5 million "shared households" in 2019**, approximately 19.8% of households, though that broader category includes nonrelatives and more complex arrangements. [8]

### Interpretation

A reasonable working range is **6-10 million** for the exact couple-plus-one-adult-child configuration. A broader parent/adult-child coresidence market would be materially larger.

This is governed multi-principal context, but it is not necessarily simple. The adult child is legally independent, even when financially dependent or living at home. HIPAA does not grant parents automatic access to an adult child's medical information merely because the adult child lives with or receives support from them. [9]

That makes it commercially relevant but architecturally more difficult than the older-parent/caregiver case.

---

## C. Single adult plus an involved adult child living elsewhere

### Best estimate

* **15-22 million recipient households**
* **11%-17% of all U.S. households**

This is an estimated household count, not a directly published Census category.

A 2025 Pew survey found:

* **10% of all U.S. adults** identify as caregivers for a parent age 65 or older.
* Among adults with a parent 65 or older, **24%** identify as caregivers.
* About **42%** of parent caregivers regularly help manage health care.
* About **39%** regularly help manage finances. [10]

Ten percent of the U.S. adult population represents roughly **26 million adult-child caregivers**. But that does not equal 26 million recipient households because:

* siblings may care for the same parent;
* one caregiver may assist two parents;
* some parents live together;
* some caregivers live with the recipient.

After adjusting for duplication and coresidence, the likely number of separate older-parent households receiving adult-child involvement is approximately **15-22 million**.

### Product relevance

This may be the cleanest compelling HIP use case:

* one older principal;
* one primary adult child;
* clear purpose;
* remote access;
* explicit delegation;
* health and financial information;
* high cost of mistakes;
* recurring information-management need.

## D. Individual plus paid or family caregiver

### Best estimate

* **25-35 million care-recipient households**
* **19%-26% of all U.S. households**

AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving estimated **63 million U.S. caregivers in 2025**, including **59 million caring for an adult**. [11]

A separate AARP analysis estimates **51 million caregivers are assisting someone age 50 or older**. [12]

Those are caregiver counts, not unique care-recipient or household counts. Multiple caregivers frequently assist the same recipient. Therefore, dividing caregivers by plausible caregiver-per-recipient ratios and adjusting for couples produces an estimated **25-35 million recipient households**.

AARP also reports that:

* **74%** of caregivers to someone 50-plus live with or within 20 minutes of the recipient.
* More than **10%** live an hour or more away.
* Remote monitoring use rose from **13% in 2020 to 25% in 2025**. [12]

This is not a niche behavior. It is a large and rapidly digitizing coordination system.

---

## E. Older person living alone with adult-child or relative oversight

### Best estimate

* **4-8 million households with active caregiving or oversight**
* **3%-6% of all U.S. households**
* Larger potential population of older adults living alone: approximately **12-15 million households**

Census data from 2020 indicated that single householders age 65 or older represented approximately **8.7% of all households**. Applied to current household totals and adjusted for aging, that implies approximately **12-15 million older-adult one-person households today**. [13]

Not all have an involved child or caregiver. Applying a plausible involvement range of approximately 35%-55% produces **4-8 million actively governed dyads**.

That involvement range is an estimate based on caregiver prevalence, not a direct national household statistic.

### Why this segment matters disproportionately

These households combine:

* physical separation;
* medication management;
* appointments;
* fraud risk;
* financial administration;
* emergency access;
* autonomy concerns;
* changing capacity;
* multiple potential helpers.

This is precisely where "one-person household" does **not** mean "one-principal information system."

---

## Other relevant two-party configurations

### Older spouse-caregiver dyads

Pew found **3% of U.S. adults** identify as caregivers for a spouse or partner age 65 or older. [10]

Estimated household segment:

* **6-8 million households**
* **5%-6% of households**

Most are already included in older-couple and caregiver estimates.

### Adult with disability plus parent, sibling, or legal representative

There is no clean national household count matching this exact governance configuration. It is embedded within the 59 million adult-caregiver count and disability statistics.

Plausible addressable segment:

* **4-8 million households**
* **3%-6%**

This is likely high-value but governance-intensive because authority may involve supported decision-making, guardianship, power of attorney, or informal family involvement.

### Close friends and nonrelative caregivers

AARP caregiving surveys include family members and friends. This is an important configuration for childless older adults and LGBTQ adults whose practical family may not be legally related.

Likely scale:

* several million households;
* no reliable nonoverlapping household count.

# 3. Eldercare and remote-care market

## Households containing older adults

The 2024 ACS estimated approximately **43.6 million households had at least one resident age 65 or older**, equal to **32.8% of U.S. households**. [1]

That includes:

* older adults living alone;
* older couples;
* older adults living with children;
* multigenerational homes;
* households with one older and one younger spouse.

A reasonable decomposition:

| Older-adult household form                           |   Estimated households |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------: |
| Older adult living alone                             |      **12-15 million** |
| Older couple / two-person older household            |      **15-20 million** |
| Older adult in larger or multigenerational household |      **10-15 million** |
| **Total with at least one person 65+**               | **43.6 million cited** |

The first three are estimated allocations and overlap at the margins; the total is the published ACS number.

## Informal caregiving

Current authoritative measurements differ because they use different definitions:

* AARP/NAC: **63 million caregivers**, approximately one in four adults, including care for adults and children with complex needs. [11]
* Adult caregivers only: approximately **59 million**. [14]
* Caregivers assisting someone age 50-plus: approximately **51 million**. [12]
* Pew's narrower self-identification measure: **10% of adults caring for a parent 65-plus** and **3% caring for a spouse or partner 65-plus**. [10]

The difference is not contradictory. AARP asks about a broader range of caregiving activities and conditions; Pew uses a narrower aging-family-member framing.

## Remote and long-distance caregiving

For caregivers of adults 50-plus:

* **74%** live with or within 20 minutes of the recipient.
* Approximately **26%** are more than 20 minutes away.
* More than **10%** are at least an hour away. [12]

Applied to 51 million caregivers:

* roughly **13 million** are more than 20 minutes away;
* at least **5 million** are an hour or more away.

Again, these are caregiver counts, not household counts.

The stronger product signal is the rise in remote technology use: caregiver remote monitoring increased from **13% to 25% between 2020 and 2025**. [15]

That is directly aligned with a governed remote-context platform.

## Sandwich generation

There are two useful definitions.

Pew found **23% of U.S. adults** were "sandwiched" between an aging parent and either a dependent minor or an adult child receiving financial support. Among adults in their 40s, the share was **54%**. [16]

AARP's 2025 caregiver survey found **29% of caregivers** were simultaneously supporting children and adults. [11]

Those figures represent:

* approximately **60 million adults** under Pew's broad demographic definition;
* approximately **18 million caregivers** under AARP's active-caregiving definition.

The second figure is more relevant to HIP because it represents actual coordination rather than potential responsibility.

# 4. Growth over the next 10-15 years

The growth case is real, but it should not be described as an unlimited demographic explosion.

## What is solid

* The U.S. population age 65 and older reached **55.8 million in 2020**, up **38.6% from 2010**. [17]
* From 2020 to 2024, the older population grew another **13%**, compared with only **1.4% growth** in the working-age population. [18]
* By 2030, all baby boomers will be 65 or older, and roughly **one in five Americans** will be of retirement age. [19]
* Older adults are projected to outnumber children during the 2030s. [20]
* Caregiving prevalence rose from roughly 43.5 million caregivers in 2015 to 63 million in 2025 under the AARP/NAC measure. [11]

## Product-relevant trajectory

Over the next decade:

1. The youngest boomers move through their late 60s and early 70s.
2. The oldest boomers move into their 80s, when disability, dementia, medication burden, and assistance needs rise sharply.
3. Adult children become more likely to coordinate care remotely.
4. Smaller families mean fewer adult children may divide the work.
5. Geographic dispersion increases dependence on digital coordination.
6. More care remains home-based because institutional care is expensive.

Pew found caregiving prevalence among people with aging relatives rises materially when the parent or spouse reaches 75-plus. Among people with an aging parent, **31% of those whose parent was 75-plus** identified as caregivers, compared with **16% when the parent was 65-74**. [10]

The most product-relevant wave is therefore not just "boomers turning 65." It is **boomers moving through 75-85 between roughly 2025 and 2045**.

# 5. Multi-principal versus genuinely single-principal households

## Directly observable multi-resident households

If multi-principal governance is defined strictly as more than one person residing in the household:

* **71% of U.S. households are multi-resident**
* approximately **94 million households**

That is simply 100% minus the 29% one-person household share. [21]

But residence is not the same as governance need.

Some multi-resident households function mostly as one administrative unit. Conversely, some single-person households have significant outside involvement.

## Functional multi-principal households

A more useful definition:

> A household in which information about one person is legitimately accessed, contributed to, managed, or acted upon by at least one other person.

Under that definition, the estimate:

* **75%-85% of U.S. households are potentially multi-principal**
* approximately **100-113 million households**

This includes:

* nearly all multi-person households;
* older adults living alone with involved relatives;
* disabled adults with external caregivers;
* separated or divorced coparents;
* adult children overseeing parents;
* individuals with powers of attorney or financial agents.

This is an estimate. No national dataset directly measures "information-governance principals."

## Genuinely single-principal households

A genuinely single-principal household would require:

* one resident;
* no spouse or partner materially involved;
* no caregiver;
* no dependent relationship;
* no adult child managing health or finances;
* no external agent or family member with emergency authority.

Starting from 29% one-person households and subtracting those with meaningful external involvement gives a plausible range of:

* **15%-25% of all households**
* approximately **20-33 million households**

The uncertainty is significant. A defensible midpoint is around **20%**.

This means the "true single-user only" market may be much smaller than the one-person-household statistic suggests.

# 6. Trust-circle size

## What the research supports

Social-network research generally finds nested layers of relationships:

* a very small support core;
* a somewhat larger sympathy or confidant circle;
* broader friendship and acquaintance networks.

Dunbar's research is commonly summarized as:

* roughly **five people** in the closest support layer;
* around **15** in a broader sympathy/confidant layer;
* much larger outer social networks. [22]

This does **not** prove that people share medical passwords, financial records, or medication information with five people.

Sensitive operational information is typically narrower and role-specific:

* spouse or partner;
* one primary adult child;
* occasionally another sibling;
* clinician;
* paid caregiver;
* financial or legal agent.

Pew's caregiving evidence supports this concentration. For older-parent caregivers, the recurring tasks are specific and operational: health management, medication, appointments, finances, errands and personal care. [10]

HHS rules similarly frame authorized health sharing around specific persons involved in care or payment, not the family generally. [23]

## Does evidence support a 1-3-person sensitive-information circle?

**Directionally, yes. Directly, not conclusively.**

The literature supports the claim that meaningful support networks are small. It does not provide a clean national statistic saying "the average American shares sensitive information with 1.8 people."

A defensible statement:

> Most recurring health, financial and caregiving coordination appears to be concentrated among a small number of role-defined people, often a spouse or one primary adult child, although broader family and support networks may exist around them.

For product architecture, designing for:

* one data subject;
* one primary trusted person;
* one optional secondary delegate;

is empirically reasonable.

But the system must support exceptions. Caregiving often becomes a small team rather than a permanent dyad.

# 7. Consolidated segment estimate

## Narrow definition

"Small trust circle" means:

* one or two central people;
* clear subject and delegate;
* explicit access;
* manageable remote coordination;
* recurring health, financial, household or care information.

### Estimated core segment

| Component                                                        | Approximate household share |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------: |
| Two-adult couples with meaningful shared administration          |                     20%-27% |
| Older parent/adult-child remote coordination not already counted |                      8%-12% |
| Spouse-caregiver and disability/caregiver dyads                  |                       5%-8% |
| Other one-person households with external oversight              |                       3%-5% |
| **Nonoverlapping modeled total**                                 |                 **45%-55%** |

This produces a core addressable segment of approximately:

* **60-73 million U.S. households**

## Broader definition

Including households that do not currently have intensive coordination but have a plausible need for governed shared context:

* ordinary couples;
* aging couples planning future delegation;
* divorced/coparenting households;
* adult children living with parents;
* households managing dependents;
* individuals with emergency contacts or powers of attorney;

the potential market rises to:

* **55%-65% of households**
* **73-86 million households**

Going materially above 65% becomes difficult to defend without defining almost any relationship as a market need.

# Honest verdict: niche or mass market?

## Demographically: mass market

A 45%-55% core demographic translates to more than 60 million households. That is not a niche.

Small-household structure, aging, caregiving, remote coordination and household complexity are all large-scale trends. The market is also present across other advanced economies.

## As a paid standalone product: not automatically mass market

The mistake would be concluding:

> "Sixty million households fit the structure, therefore sixty million will buy governed household context."

Most people do not wake up wanting:

* a context layer;
* information governance;
* principal-level encryption;
* consent routing;
* household memory infrastructure.

They want:

* help managing their mother's medications;
* confidence that siblings see the right information;
* fewer missed appointments;
* protection from scams;
* a reliable emergency record;
* continuity when a caregiver changes;
* control over what an AI or service provider can access.

The **urgent initial market** is probably the eldercare, chronic-care, disability and high-administrative-burden subset:

* approximately **20-33 million households**
* roughly **15%-25% of U.S. households**

That is still a very large market.

## Strategic interpretation for HIP

The data supports the multi-principal thesis, but it does **not** support building a generalized family-governance system for every possible household configuration first.

The strongest initial wedge:

> **One person whose information must remain controlled, plus one explicitly authorized spouse, adult child, relative, or caregiver who assists locally or remotely.**

That configuration is:

* widespread;
* easy to explain;
* associated with real pain;
* small enough to govern;
* naturally suited to explicit delegation;
* compatible with operator-hosted home infrastructure;
* clearly differentiated from ordinary single-user AI memory.

The market evidence therefore favors a **dyad-first multi-principal product**, not a single-user product and not a fully generalized household-governance product.

**Bottom line:** HIP's realistic target is not every American household. It is initially about **one household in five with an active care or oversight need**, expanding toward **roughly half of households** where a small, governed trust circle has practical value.
